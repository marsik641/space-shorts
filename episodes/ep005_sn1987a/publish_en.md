## Description
```
On February 23, 1987, detectors in Japan, the USA and Russia caught a burst of neutrinos from a star that had exploded 168,000 light-years away — hours before its light arrived.
The neutrinos came up through the Earth: for Kamiokande-II in Japan, the supernova was below the horizon, so they crossed about 4,300 km of rock first.

Visualization based on real data:
• Progenitor Sanduleak −69° 202: blue supergiant, ~20 solar masses, ~40× the Sun's radius (IAUC 4317; Menon & Heger 2017)
• Burning stages of a 15–20-solar-mass star (models): hydrogen ~8–11 million years, silicon ~11–18 days (Heger et al. 2003; Woosley & Janka 2005)
• Iron core ~1.5 solar masses, "the size of the Earth", collapses in under a second at ~¼ the speed of light into a proto-neutron star ~60 km across, which shrinks to a neutron star ~20–25 km across within seconds; ~10⁵⁸ neutrinos carry ~99% of the energy (Woosley & Janka 2005; Nobel Prize 2002)
• Shock breakout on a blue supergiant ~2 h after collapse (models 1.3–2.2 h; Blinnikov et al. 2000); light first seen 1.8–3 h after the neutrinos (IAUC 4316, 4340)
• Kamiokande-II, 23 Feb 1987 07:35:35 UT: 12 events in 12.4 s; the collaboration counted 11 as supernova neutrinos (one is likely background), the Nobel Committee cites twelve; event times from Hirata et al. 1988 (as tabulated in Fiorillo et al. 2023); supernova 19.7° below the horizon → ~4,300 km through the Earth (Fiorillo et al. 2023)
• Kamiokande-II: 3,000 t of water under ~1 km of rock, 948 twenty-inch photomultipliers; each event is a ring of Cherenkov light at ~41° (IAUC 4338; IEEE Milestone)
• Distance 51.4 ± 1.2 kpc ≈ 168,000 light-years from the ring geometry (Panagia 2003)
• Peak V ≈ 3.0 in May 1987 — the first naked-eye supernova since Kepler's in 1604 (NASA/ESA)
• Inner ring: radius 0.66 light-years, tilted ~44°, shed ~20,000 years before the explosion; outer rings ~2.2× larger (Panagia 2003; Crotts & Heathcote 2000; Tziamtzis et al. 2011)
• Debris hit the ring from 1995: 26 hot spots by 2022, brightest around 2009, now fading (France et al. 2010; Fransson et al. 2015; Tegkelidis et al. 2024)
• JWST 2024: ionized argon at the center — most likely a neutron star, still hidden in dust (Fransson et al. 2024)
```
